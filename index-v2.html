Pitch, frequency, harmonics and shruti

Pitch

Pitch is how high or low a sound is perceived. For a steady voiced sound it is closely related to the fundamental frequency, but perception also depends on timbre and context.

Frequency

Frequency counts vibrations per second (Hz). Doubling the frequency raises the pitch by one octave, so pitch intervals are ratios, not differences.

Harmonics

A voice or string vibrates at a fundamental and at whole-number multiples of it. These harmonics shape timbre and are one reason pitch detectors can make octave errors.

Shruti

In traditional theory, shruti refers to the smallest audible pitch distinctions used in music; classical texts speak of 22 per octave. How those relate to measurable frequencies is debated, so this tool does not claim a single answer.

Research project overview

Research question: How reliably can a browser-based pitch estimator measure sustained vocal tones, and how do background noise and room reverberation affect its accuracy and stability?

✔ Implemented now

  • Real microphone pitch detection (YIN-based pipeline) and an autocorrelation baseline
  • Audible reference-tone generator (Web Audio oscillators)
  • Swara mapping under three documented interval models
  • Pitch-stability recording with statistics and CSV export
  • Interrupted-noise decay-time estimator with synthetic self-check
  • Reproducible synthetic-tone validation with seeded noise
  • Version 2: a drone-aware pitch tracker (calibrated drone-spectrum subtraction) and a seeded simulation study with known ground truth (drone, noise, reverberation) with CSV/WAV export

◔ To be investigated experimentally

  • Whether confidence-based rejection plus smoothing reduces errors vs. the baseline on real sustained vocal tones
  • Effect of background noise levels on accuracy and stability
  • Effect of room reverberation on pitch estimates
  • Whether drone-aware tracking and room-adaptive settings help on real recordings, not just in simulation
  • Whether any contribution is genuinely novel (requires prior-art search — nothing is claimed)

Important: the seven swaras do not have one universal set of fixed frequencies. Their sounding pitch depends on the tonic Sa you choose, the tuning convention, the raga, and the performer's practice. The numbers here come from a documented simplified model. The equal-tempered option is an approximation, not the only correct model of Indian classical music, and real performances include microtonal inflections (such as gamak and meend) that a table of seven numbers cannot capture.

Seven swaras vs. twelve pitch positions

Within one octave, Hindustani music commonly uses twelve pitch positions: seven shuddha (natural) swaras plus lowered (komal) Re, Ga, Dha, Ni and a raised (tivra) Ma. Sa and Pa are achala (immovable). The seven names are therefore a naming framework, not seven isolated pitches. Selected-model values for all twelve positions:

About notation, ratios and the models

Notation: capital letters = shuddha (S, R, G, M, P, D, N); lowercase = komal (r, g, d, n); M with a prime / "Tivra Ma" = raised Ma. Cents are a logarithmic unit: 1200 cents = one octave, 100 cents = one equal-tempered semitone.

12-TET divides the octave into twelve equal steps (ratio 2i/12). Just intonation uses small-integer ratios such as 3/2 for Pa and 5/4 for Ga; several variants exist in the literature and this is one simplified table. Pythagorean builds all pitches from stacked 3/2 fifths. None of these is presented as "the" Indian tuning.

Drone-aware mode Experimental · v2

A plain pitch tracker locks onto the loudest periodic sound — with a tanpura running, that is often the drone, not your voice. The drone-aware mode first learns what the drone alone sounds like (calibration), then subtracts that spectrum from what the microphone hears before estimating pitch.

  1. Start a drone: your own tanpura / shruti box, or press Play drone (synthetic Sa–Pa tanpura-like sound at the current Sa). Use the laptop speakers, not headphones, so the microphone can hear it.
  2. Stay silent and press Calibrate (3 s). The microphone turns on if it is off.
  3. The algorithm switches to “Drone-aware”. Now sing or hum over the drone and watch the readout.
  4. Compare by switching the algorithm menu below to “Improved” or “Baseline” while you sing the same note.

Not calibrated.

Limits: the drone must stay about the same during calibration and singing (same loudness, same microphone position, same Sa). If you change Sa, volume or move the microphone, calibrate again. If your voice is much quieter than the drone, nothing is left after subtraction and the tracker reports no pitch. This is a research prototype whose benefit is being measured in the Simulation Study; it is not claimed to work for every recording.

About the 22 shrutis

Traditional theory

Classical Indian music theory (for example in the Natyashastra tradition) describes 22 shrutis within an octave. Scholars interpret what those 22 intervals are — their exact sizes, how they map to the swaras, and whether they describe distinct tuned pitches or a framework of possible inflections — in different ways.

This website does not assign measured frequencies to 22 shrutis and does not claim any single interpretation is correct. The three models above are simplified educational tools.

3. Reverberation (decay-time) experiment

Method: the app plays a short white-noise burst through your speakers, records the room with your microphone, then estimates the decay slope after the burst ends (a T20-style fit: −5 dB to −25 dB, extrapolated to −60 dB). This follows the idea of the interrupted-noise method used in room acoustics.

Not calibrated. Laptop speakers, microphone response, distance, level and background noise all bias the result. The value is an uncalibrated estimate useful for comparing conditions on the same equipment; it must not be presented as the true reverberation time of a room or temple. If the signal does not exceed the noise floor by at least 35 dB, the app reports "not measurable" instead of a number.

Concepts: what is being measured?

Pitch accuracy

How close an estimate is to a reference value (ground truth), e.g. error in cents. It can only be computed when a trustworthy reference exists, such as a generated tone.

Pitch stability

How much the estimate fluctuates over time for a nominally steady tone (standard deviation in cents). A very stable estimate can still be inaccurate, and a singer's natural vibrato appears as instability.

Noise

Unwanted sound (fans, traffic, electronics). It lowers the signal-to-noise ratio and can cause missed or wrong detections.

Reverberation

Persistence of sound after the source stops, due to reflections. Summarised by decay time such as RT60. Reverb smears successive notes and can blur pitch estimates.

Room acoustics

The overall effect of room size, shape and materials: reverberation, echoes, resonant modes and background noise. Measuring a specific space such as a temple would need calibrated equipment and validation, which this prototype does not claim.

Simulation Study · Version 2

Drone, noise and room: a controlled pitch-tracker comparison

Generates a sung melody with a known true pitch, mixes in a tanpura-like drone, background noise and a simulated room, and scores four pitch trackers against the truth. Everything is seeded, so the same settings reproduce the same numbers.

Scope. This is a computer-simulated study. The "voice" is a synthetic harmonic source, the "drone" is a synthetic plucked-string model and the "room" is a random exponentially-decaying impulse response. Results show how the algorithms behave under those assumptions. They are not evidence about real singers, real tanpuras or real rooms — that requires recordings (Acoustic Lab, Live Visualizer drone mode, or the exported audio files). No result is stored in this page: every number you see comes from the run you start below.

H1 · inherited from v1

Smoothing + confidence rejection

The improved v1 pipeline (YIN, confidence rejection, smoothing) has higher raw pitch accuracy or fewer unstable detections than the baseline autocorrelation, under selected conditions.

H2 · new

Drone-aware front end

When a drone sounds together with the voice, subtracting a calibrated drone spectrum before pitch estimation raises raw pitch accuracy and lowers drone false alarms compared with the improved v1 pipeline.

H3 · new

Room-adaptive settings

Setting smoothing and confidence from the measured decay time (interrupted-noise method) improves accuracy in reverberant rooms compared with fixed settings.

Each is a hypothesis to test; any of them can be rejected. Null hypotheses: no difference between the two methods being compared.

How the scoring works

  • Frame truth: a frame counts as voiced if ≥ 90% of its samples fall inside a sung note, and as a rest if ≤ 10%. Frames in between (note edges) are ignored. The true pitch of a voiced frame is the mean true pitch inside it (glides and vibrato included).
  • Raw pitch accuracy (RPA): share of voiced frames whose estimate is within 50 cents (RPA50) or 20 cents (RPA20) of the truth. A missing estimate counts as a miss — the usual convention for melody-extraction evaluation.
  • Drone false alarm: share of rest frames in which the tracker still reports a pitch (for a plain tracker this is usually the drone itself, or the reverberant tail).
  • Octave errors: voiced frames whose estimate is off by about one or more octaves. Median |error| is taken over frames where a pitch was reported.
  • Metrics are fractions of frames in 7.3 s of melody (six notes and two rests). Detailed rows are exportable; use the Python scripts for formal statistics.

Research & Experiments

    
A testable study of browser-based pitch estimation

    
This page describes a study design. It contains no results: any numbers must come from your own measurements in the Acoustic Lab and Validation Tests.

  

  

    

      
Research motivation
Students and teachers increasingly use phone and laptop apps to check pitch while practising. Indian classical music values microtonal nuance and sustained tones, yet it is rarely documented how dependable a lightweight in-browser estimator is for such tones, and how room and noise conditions change that dependability.

      
Problem statement
Simple pitch estimators can jump by octaves, report values during silence, or fluctuate under noise and reverberation. Without measuring these failure modes, an on-screen reading may mislead learners.

    

    
Research question
How reliably can a browser-based pitch estimator measure sustained vocal tones, and how do background noise and room reverberation affect its accuracy and stability?

    
Testable hypothesis (to be tested, not an established result)
An improved pitch-estimation pipeline combining confidence-based rejection and temporal smoothing will reduce pitch-estimation errors or unstable detections compared with a baseline estimator under selected recording conditions.

      
Null hypothesis: there is no difference in error or instability between the two pipelines under those conditions. The hypothesis could be supported, partly supported, or rejected.

    
Additional hypotheses added in version 2 (to be tested)

      
H2 — drone-aware front end. When a drone sounds together with the voice, estimating pitch after subtracting a calibrated drone spectrum gives higher raw pitch accuracy and fewer drone false alarms than the improved v1 pipeline. Null: no difference.

      
H3 — room-adaptive settings. Choosing smoothing and confidence settings from a measured decay time improves accuracy in reverberant rooms compared with fixed settings. Null: no difference.

      
Where to test: Simulation Study (known ground truth, seeded), then real recordings (Live Visualizer drone mode, Acoustic Lab) and the exported WAV files with an external tracker. Simulation results do not transfer automatically to real singing; they only tell you which ideas deserve a real-recording experiment. Both H2 and H3 can be rejected, and a rejected hypothesis is still a valid result to report.


    

      
Independent variables
  • Estimator: baseline vs. improved
  • Background-noise condition (quiet / moderate / noisy)
  • Room condition (e.g. small furnished room / hard-surfaced room)
  • Signal type: generated tone vs. sustained vocal tone
  • Fundamental frequency (e.g. Sa at different tonics)

      
Dependent variables
  • Cents error vs. reference (when a reference exists)
  • Pitch stability: SD of cents over a sustained tone
  • Voiced fraction (detections per frame)
  • Jumps >100 ¢ and octave jumps per recording
  • Estimated decay time (uncalibrated) as a covariate

      
Controlled variables
  • Same laptop, microphone and browser
  • Fixed microphone distance and position
  • Browser audio processing off
  • Same frame size, sample rate, gate and settings (record algorithm version shruti-pitch/1.0.0)
  • Same vowel and vocal effort; same duration

Baseline method
Plain autocorrelation: the first autocorrelation peak reaching 90% of the maximum within the lag range 1/1000 s – 1/70 s, integer-lag resolution, no confidence rejection, no smoothing (the same silence gate is applied to both methods so the comparison isolates the pitch logic).

      
Proposed improvement
YIN-style cumulative-mean-normalised difference function (de Cheveigné & Kawahara, 2002) with parabolic interpolation, a minimum-confidence requirement, suspension of sudden >600 ¢ jumps until confirmed on consecutive frames (octave-error guard), a short median filter and log-domain exponential smoothing.

    


    

      
Data-collection methodology
    
        
  • All measurements are produced by this application and exported as CSV (one row per run per algorithm).
    • 
        
  • Each row stores timestamp, algorithm version, settings, condition labels and statistics.
    • 
        
  • Human vocal tones have no built-in ground truth. Options: ask the participant to match a generated reference tone; or compare estimators against each other and against an independent offline analysis (a limitation to state explicitly).
    • 
        
  • Obtain consent from any participant; no audio is stored or transmitted.

      
Planned statistical analysis
    
        
  • Descriptive statistics per condition: median, mean, SD, IQR of cents error and stability.
    • 
        
  • Paired comparison of baseline vs. improved on the same recordings: Wilcoxon signed-rank test (distributions are unlikely to be normal), with effect size.
    • 
        
  • Bootstrap confidence intervals for median differences.
    • 
        
  • Condition effects (noise, room): non-parametric or mixed-effects models, with correction for multiple comparisons.
    • 
        
  • Report all conditions, including those where the hypothesis fails.

    


    
Limitations
    
      
  • Single-device, single-browser measurements do not generalise to other microphones or rooms.
    • 
      
  • Autocorrelation/YIN-type methods assume one dominant periodic source; they are not designed for polyphonic audio (e.g. voice with a loud tanpura).
    • 
      
  • Rapid gamak and meend, vibrato and breathiness make "the" pitch ill-defined; stability SD then reflects the music, not only the error.
    • 
      
  • The decay-time estimate is uncalibrated and depends on speaker, microphone and noise.
    • 
      
  • 12-TET, just intonation and Pythagorean tables are simplified models; they do not settle debates about shruti.


    
Potential Technical Contribution — Pending Validation

      
The pipeline combines components that are individually well known (YIN, confidence thresholds, median filtering, smoothing). Any genuine novelty, if it exists at all, would have to come from a specific combination, a specific adaptation to sustained Indian vocal practice, or a specific evaluation protocol — and that is not established.

      
Candidate (version 2): subtracting a calibrated drone spectrum before YIN-style pitch estimation, for sustained Indian vocal music, evaluated under drone, noise and reverberation. The individual ingredients (spectral subtraction, noise profiles, YIN) are well known, and the Indian-music literature already studies drone-related issues such as tonic identification and predominant-melody extraction, so overlap with existing work is likely. Whether any combination is new is unknown.

      
Read first and search around (verify each source yourself): pYIN — probabilistic threshold distributions for F0 estimation · Automatic tonic identification in Indian art music: approaches and evaluation · A two-stage approach for tonic identification in Indian art music. Suggested search terms: “drone suppression”, “tanpura”, “pitch tracking Hindustani”, “predominant melody extraction accompaniment”, “spectral subtraction fundamental frequency”, “noise-robust pitch estimation calibration”.

      
No prior-art search has been performed by this tool. No novelty, patentability or performance-superiority claim is made. Establishing novelty and patentability requires a separate, systematic prior-art search and professional (patent attorney / supervisor) assessment, and the hypothesis above must first be tested with real data.

About the project

    
Indian Classical Music Shruti Visualizer

    
Exploring Swaras, Pitch Accuracy, and Acoustic Effects — an Indian Knowledge System (IKS) student research project.

  

  

    

      
Indian Knowledge System domain
Indian music theory (swara, shruti, raga, tala) — a knowledge tradition with a long history of describing sound in terms of relationships. This project connects that vocabulary to modern, measurable audio analysis.

        
Connection to modern audio analysis
A sung swara is a pitch relationship; digitally it is a fundamental frequency that can be estimated, compared with a reference, and tracked over time. Signal processing helps quantify what learners and teachers hear, while recognising what such measurements cannot capture.

      
Intended users
  • Students learning swaras
  • Beginners practising pitch with a reference Sa
  • Music learners and teachers
  • Researchers needing a transparent, reproducible baseline tool

        
Technology stack
HTML5, CSS3, plain JavaScript (ES2020), Web Audio API (OscillatorNode, AnalyserNode, MediaStreamSource), Canvas 2D. A single file, with no frameworks, no backend and no network requests.

      
Current working features
  • Swara frequency mapping under three documented models
  • Reference-tone generator and swara sequences
  • Live microphone pitch detection: YIN-based pipeline + autocorrelation baseline
  • Cents deviation, target comparison, pitch trace
  • Interval explorer and ratio calculator
  • Pitch-stability recording, decay-time estimate, CSV export
  • Seeded synthetic validation tests
  • Version 2: drone-aware tracking and a seeded drone/noise/room simulation study with external-tool export

      
Known limitations
  • Monophonic voice/instrument only; a loud drone will disturb detection.
  • Equal-tempered and other tables are simplified models; no claim to define shruti.
  • Accuracy depends on microphone, room and noise; estimates are not ground truth.
  • Decay-time estimate is uncalibrated.
  • No raga-aware analysis and no per-swara adaptive tuning.

        
Future research directions
  • Controlled study of baseline vs. improved estimators on real recordings
  • Reference-assisted measurement with independent hardware tuners
  • Drone-tolerant and gamak-aware analysis
  • Calibrated room-acoustics measurement for temples and halls
  • Prior-art review before making any novelty claim

    

    
This is a student project. It has no official endorsement from any institution, and the YIN algorithm cited here is the work of de Cheveigné & Kawahara (2002), "YIN, a fundamental frequency estimator for speech and music", J. Acoust. Soc. Am.
